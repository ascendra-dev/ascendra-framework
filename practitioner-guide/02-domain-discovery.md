# Domain Discovery

## Purpose

Every project needs a domain knowledge document before it can run BRD discovery — `/gen-brd-playbook` hard-gates on one existing. That document is the layer that separates "true about this domain in every implementation" from "true about this specific client's requirements." Get the separation right and the client-facing discovery session that follows is fast, focused, and asks only the questions that actually vary. Get it wrong — universal facts treated as unknowns, or client-specific quirks treated as domain law — and the damage compounds through every phase downstream: a wrong assumption baked in here doesn't get re-examined at BRD stage, because the whole point of domain knowledge is that the BRD *doesn't* re-ask what's already established.

That's why this phase exists as a distinct step rather than folding straight into BRD discovery, and why getting the judgment calls right here matters more than the phase's modest size suggests.

Domain knowledge is generated by [`/gen-domain-knowledge`](../.claude/commands/gen-domain-knowledge.md). It can be generated two ways: straight from AI training knowledge, or primed by an optional discovery sub-flow — [`/gen-domain-playbook`](../.claude/commands/gen-domain-playbook.md) → [`/run-domain-discovery`](../.claude/commands/run-domain-discovery.md) — where the PO teaches the AI the domain before it writes anything. [`/run-mock-discovery`](../.claude/commands/run-mock-discovery.md) is a later, optional validation step that stress-tests the domain document together with the BRD playbook once both exist. The architecture behind this whole structure — why the sub-flow is optional, what each command produces — is recorded in [`FW-013`](../decisions/FW-013-domain-discovery-architecture.md).

One correction to how `SDLC.md` frames this, worth flagging up front: the AI-knowledge-only path is not "skip domain knowledge" — `/gen-domain-knowledge` itself is never optional, because `/gen-brd-playbook` requires the file it produces to exist. What's optional is only the sub-flow that *feeds* it. Read "skip straight to BRD playbook" as "skip the discovery session, not the document."

## The central decision: do you need the sub-flow?

`/gen-domain-knowledge` will happily generate a document from AI training knowledge alone, and for a lot of domains that's the right call — running a discovery session on something the AI already knows cold just burns a session for no gain. But the command's own warning only names two example buckets — "well-known" (invoicing, payroll, school fees, HR) and "niche/regulated/unfamiliar" — and most real domains don't announce which bucket they're in. "HR" sounds well-known right up until it's *HR leave accrual with jurisdiction-specific statutory entitlements*, which is a different animal entirely.

Use this test instead of the bucket list. Ask yourself three questions about the domain in front of you:

1. **Can you name the three most consequential business rules in this domain from memory, with real confidence — not "I think so"?**
2. **Would a wrong assumption here cost more than a sprint to unwind** once it's propagated into entities, lifecycle states, or BRD requirements downstream?
3. **Is there a regulator, auditor, or legal requirement that would reject wrong output** — not just annoy a user, but create real exposure?

**Any single "no" or "unsure" answer means run the sub-flow.** All three need to be a confident yes before you skip straight to `/gen-domain-knowledge` with AI-knowledge-only. This is stricter than it sounds on purpose: the cost of running an unnecessary discovery session is one afternoon; the cost of skipping a necessary one is a domain document that looks authoritative and isn't, silently feeding every phase after it.

### Worked across the spectrum

**Internal expense reimbursement tool — skip the sub-flow.**
- *Three rules from memory, confidently?* Yes: receipts required above a threshold, manager-approval chain, reimbursement capped at the submitted receipt amount. These are stable, well-worn patterns.
- *Wrong assumption costs >1 sprint?* No — it's an internal tool with a small blast radius; a wrong workflow assumption is a config change, not a rebuild.
- *Regulator/auditor exposure?* Generally no, for a purely internal domestic tool.
All three clear → proceed straight to `/gen-domain-knowledge`, AI-knowledge-only.

**Retail loyalty & points program — genuinely ambiguous, walk it through.**
- *Three rules from memory, confidently?* This is where it gets interesting. You can probably name the concepts — points accrue on spend, tiers unlock perks, points expire eventually. But can you state with confidence whether expiry is rolling-from-earn or anniversary-date, whether tier status is recalculated monthly or annually, or what happens to points on a refunded order? Most POs will find at least one of these is a guess, not a fact.
- *Wrong assumption costs >1 sprint?* Plausibly yes — if the expiry model gets baked into the ledger entity design as a Universal Business Rule rather than confirmed as a variation, correcting it later means touching the points-ledger schema and every calculation that reads it.
- *Regulator exposure?* Genuinely "maybe" — a number of jurisdictions treat unredeemed points as a breakage-accounting or unclaimed-property question, which is a real compliance surface most POs won't have front-of-mind.
This domain fails at least one question — often two — so it should run the sub-flow, even though a PO who's shipped e-commerce before will feel like they already know loyalty programs. That instinct is exactly the trap the test is designed to catch: partial fluency reads as confidence until you're asked to state the actual rule.

**Healthcare compliance or cross-border payments — clearly niche.**
- *Three rules from memory, confidently?* No — most POs cannot state, say, HIPAA's minimum-necessary-use boundary or the specific AML/CTF threshold triggers for cross-border transfers without looking them up.
- *Wrong assumption costs >1 sprint?* Yes, unambiguously — these facts get embedded in entities, lifecycle gating, and audit requirements that ripple through architecture.
- *Regulator exposure?* Yes, explicitly and by design.
Run the sub-flow, and — see the confidence-score section below — treat 70% as insufficient once you're through it.

## If you run the sub-flow: density and judgment through the playbook and live session

`/gen-domain-playbook` generates the interview guide; `/run-domain-discovery` runs the session against it and writes `domain-discovery-state.md`. The quality of what comes out is governed less by the mechanics (both commands are well-specified procedurally) and more by four judgment calls that the framework doesn't make for you.

### The single highest-stakes call in this phase: Section 5 vs. Section 10

This is the one worth understanding before you touch either document, because the framework does not warn you about it anywhere in the command files themselves, and the failure is silent.

The domain knowledge template has two places for a business rule to live: [Section 5, Universal Business Rules](../projects/TEMPLATE/domain/domain.template.md) ("apply in every implementation... not confirmed with the client") and Section 10, Known Variations ("the things you MUST ask the client about — they cannot be assumed"). Here is the mechanism that makes the choice between them high-stakes rather than cosmetic: `/gen-brd-playbook` generates its entire Section 4 (Core Discovery Questions) **only from Section 10**. Every known variation gets at least one discovery question built for it. Sections 5 and 6 generate **zero** discovery questions — by design, because a Universal Business Rule is defined as something you don't ask the client about.

So walk the failure chain through a concrete case. Suppose during domain discovery you classify "invoices above a threshold require Director approval before sending" as a Universal Business Rule instead of a Known Variation — plausible, because a lot of B2B invoicing systems do have some approval concept. Once it's in Section 5:

1. `/gen-brd-playbook` never generates a question about approval workflow, because Section 5 doesn't feed question generation.
2. The real client discovery session runs to completion without anyone asking "does your organization require approval before sending an invoice?"
3. If this particular client doesn't need approval at all, that fact surfaces — if it surfaces — only when the client happens to spontaneously contradict something you've assumed, and even then the current design treats that as a scope risk to negotiate, not a signal that the domain document's classification was wrong to begin with.
4. Worse: if the client *does* want approval but with a different shape than what got hard-coded as "universal" (single approver vs. multi-level, different threshold), there's no mechanism that ever asks — the BRD is generated against a fact that was never actually true.

Compare the cost of the mistake in the other direction: you classify something genuinely universal — say, "an invoice must have at least one line item" — as a Known Variation instead of a UBR. The downstream cost is exactly one redundant question in the BRD discovery session, probably answered by the client in one sentence, no harm done.

**The rule:** when you are not certain a rule is universal, put it in Section 10. A variation that turns out to be universal costs one redundant question. A Universal Business Rule that turns out to be variable costs a missed requirement that will not reliably surface on its own.

### The generalization filter

Section 4 of the domain playbook (Domain Scope) requires you to separate genuinely universal domain concepts from this-client's-specific product decisions that happen to be sitting in the brief. This is arguably the hardest cognitive task in the whole flow, and the template ships with exactly one worked example — walk through it, then apply the same move twice more.

**The existing example, from [`domain-playbook.example.md`](../projects/TEMPLATE/domain/domain-playbook.example.md):** the brief mentions "tiered template options with full customization" for invoice templating. The generic, universal capability is "invoice templating" — every invoice system in this domain needs some way to format an invoice. The *tiering model* (which customization level a client gets, presumably tied to a pricing plan) is not a domain truth; it's a project-specific product decision. The filter's output: list "invoice templating capability" in Section 4 (Domain Scope), and route "tiering/customization model" to Section 12 (Known Variations) as something to confirm with *this* client — not silently drop it, and not silently treat it as if every implementation has tiered templates.

**Worked example 2 — retail loyalty, points expiry.** Suppose the brief says "points expire 12 months after the customer's last purchase, rolling forward on each new transaction." The generic domain concept is "points expiry policy" — virtually every points program has some expiry mechanism. The specific mechanic — a rolling 12-month window keyed to last-purchase-date, as opposed to a fixed anniversary date or no expiry at all — is this client's particular design choice, not a domain fact. Filter output: "points expiry policy" is a universal concept (belongs in the domain document's scope, possibly with a lifecycle note that points balances can expire); "12-month rolling, last-purchase-triggered" goes into Known Variations as the specific option this client happened to describe, alongside the other common options (fixed calendar-year expiry, no expiry, tier-dependent expiry).

**Worked example 3 — HR leave accrual.** Suppose the brief says "employees accrue 1.5 days of annual leave per month, capped at 18 days carried forward into the next year." The generic domain concept is "leave accrual" — every leave-management domain has some accrual mechanism, with a UBR-level truth like "leave balance cannot be used before it accrues." The specific accrual rate, monthly cadence, and 18-day carry-forward cap are this client's policy, not domain law — different clients (and different jurisdictions' statutory minimums) will have entirely different numbers and cadences. Filter output: "leave accrual mechanism" and "carry-forward capability" belong in the domain document's scope and entity model; the specific rate and cap go to Known Variations.

The pattern in all three: if a number, a named tier, or a specific policy choice in the brief could plausibly be different for another client in the same domain, it's a variation wearing a universal-sounding sentence. Generalize the concept, route the specifics.

### Sub-domain decomposition — when to split before you start

[`FW-014`](../decisions/FW-014-subdomain-decomposition.md) recommends splitting a domain into multiple documents when it spans more than one distinct user-facing portal or independent capability area — but "admin portal vs. parent portal" is an easy call, and most real ambiguity lives well below that bar (is Loyalty & Rewards its own sub-domain from Order Management? Is Dunning its own sub-domain from Invoicing?).

Two practical tests, one to apply before you start and one to catch it if you missed it:

- **Before you start — the 2-3 sentence test:** try to describe the domain's scope in 2-3 sentences without joining two user roles, or two lifecycle-independent entities, with "and." If you can't do it without an "and" doing real work ("...covers order management **and** loyalty point redemption..."), that "and" is usually the seam — split there.
- **After you've started — the shallow-session tell:** if a discovery session is running long and the answers you're getting feel thin or generic rather than sharp and specific, that's a live symptom of having already split too late. A well-scoped single sub-domain produces deep, confident answers because the domain expert (you, or whoever you're interviewing) is reasoning about one coherent thing. Shallow answers across a long session are the domain telling you it's actually two domains being asked about at once — stop, split, and run two focused sessions instead of finishing one diffuse one.

### "Covered" and "Unverified" — read past the label

The discovery-state file and the generated domain document each carry a status marker that looks authoritative and isn't, on its own.

**"Covered" in `domain-discovery-state.md`** means a section was addressed in the session — it does not mean it was addressed *well*. A rushed "yes, that all sounds right" from a tired PO at the end of a long session can get marked Covered exactly the same way a detailed, specific answer does. Don't trust the word "Covered" by itself. Open the section and check whether there's a substantive "PO stated:" paragraph behind it — the real content, in the PO's own terms, with specifics — or just a one-line rubber stamp. [`domain-discovery-state.example.md`](../projects/TEMPLATE/domain/domain-discovery-state.example.md) shows what a real one looks like: the Section 6 entry doesn't just say "lifecycle confirmed," it records the specific correction (Overdue vs. Overdue (Escalated) as genuinely distinct states) with the reasoning behind it. That's the bar. A "Covered" section that's a sentence long with no specifics is functionally still open.

**"Unverified" in the generated domain document's Section 12.3 (Factual Accuracy Log)** marks a fact — usually regulatory or time-sensitive — that the AI could not confirm against an authoritative source. The label's entire enforcement mechanism is the string itself in a table cell. Nothing blocks the document from being used downstream while an Unverified fact sits there, and the closing report from `/gen-domain-knowledge` doesn't surface an "Unverified: N" count the way it does for Open Issues. Practical consequence: you have to personally scan Section 12.3 before trusting the document, every time — it will not be flagged for you elsewhere.

## The Domain Confidence Score, and what it doesn't tell you

`run-domain-discovery.md` and `domain-discovery-state.template.md` both instruct the AI to "estimate what percentage of the domain knowledge document can be generated with confidence" after each session, gated at a fixed 70% before generation is recommended. Two things worth knowing before you lean on this number.

**First: the formula is not published, and you cannot reproduce it.** The worked example in `domain-discovery-state.example.md` shows 55% after 6 of 10 tracked sections were marked Covered — a naive section-count ratio would put that at 60%, so the score is undershooting the naive ratio, not matching it. The most plausible explanation, reading the state file's own language ("**Critical gap.** This section directly feeds the BRD playbook"), is that Section 12 (Known Variations) carries outsized weight relative to a flat per-section average — consistent with the Section 5-vs-10 stakes discussed above. But this is inference, not documentation; there's no published weighting to check a number against. Treat any score you're handed as a rough directional signal, not a measurement you could audit.

**The practical proxy:** don't try to reverse-engineer the math. Instead, personally weight your own confidence the way the evidence suggests the score does — read Section 12 (Known Variations) and Section 9 (Regulatory Baseline) yourself, in full, regardless of what the percentage says, before you trust the document. A 70% score built on a complete Section 12 and a thin Section 7 (Personas) is in much better shape than a 70% built the other way around, and the single number can't tell you which one you're looking at.

**Second, and more important: 70% is not a sufficient bar for a regulated or high-stakes domain, and nothing in the framework raises it for you automatically.** The gate is fixed at 70% regardless of what the domain is. For an internal tool, 70% with a couple of gaps in Personas or Integrations is a fine place to start generating from — those gaps get closed easily during BRD discovery anyway. For a regulated or high-stakes domain (the same domains your self-test above told you to run the sub-flow for in the first place), apply a personal floor of **90%**, and — regardless of what the score says — personally re-read Section 9 (Regulatory Baseline) and Section 12.3 (Factual Accuracy Log) line by line before treating the document as production-ready. The framework will let you generate at 70% either way; it will not tell you that 70% means something very different depending on what's at stake. See `00-orientation.md`'s note on scores-and-thresholds generally — this is the domain-phase instance of that pattern.

## Mock discovery's role and its blind spot

Once a domain document and a BRD playbook both exist, `/run-mock-discovery` runs an internal simulation: the same model plays both the facilitator (following the BRD playbook) and a simulated client (answering using the domain knowledge document), then grades the result against five checks — redundant questions, missed variations, restated defaults, missing requirements, factual errors. It's a genuinely useful gate, and it's cheap to run, so treat it as close to mandatory before any real client session on a domain you ran the sub-flow for.

But be precise about what a clean pass actually proves. **The model grading the session is the same model that wrote the domain knowledge document and the BRD playbook being tested.** If there's a gap in Section 10 (Known Variations) that the AI simply didn't think to include — a variation real clients in this domain commonly hit, but that isn't part of this model's training-knowledge picture of the domain — the simulated client won't probe for it either, for the same underlying reason it wasn't in Section 10 to begin with. Same blind spot, same source, checking itself. A 5/5 pass is real evidence that the domain document and the BRD playbook are *internally consistent with each other* — it is not evidence that they're *complete against the real world*, and the gap between those two things is exactly where a niche domain's actual risk lives. Don't let a clean mock-discovery pass talk you out of a domain expert's own read of Section 10 on a domain you already judged niche enough to need the sub-flow for.

## Terminology recap

This phase doesn't introduce anything not already in `00-orientation.md`'s terminology map, but two distinctions are worth having sharp before you run it:

- **Universal Business Rule (Section 5) vs. Known Variation (Section 10)** — not a stylistic choice, a routing decision with the downstream consequence described above. When in doubt, Section 10.
- **Content Status vs. Status** — the gate check in `/gen-domain-knowledge` (Step 1.5) checks the brief's *Content Status*, not *Status*; the distinction between the two fields is explained in `brief.template.md`, not in the domain command itself, so if you hit the gate and are confused which field it means, that's expected — see `01-intake.md` for the full explanation.

## Common mistakes

- Treating "HR," "invoicing," or another named-in-the-warning domain as automatically well-known without running the three-question test — the warning's example list is a starting point, not a verdict.
- Classifying an uncertain rule as universal (Section 5) because it "sounds like it's always true" — when genuinely unsure, it belongs in Section 10.
- Trusting a domain document's confidence score number without personally reading Section 12 (Known Variations) and, on a regulated domain, Section 9 and 12.3.
- Marking a discovery-state section "Covered" on a blanket "yes to all" rather than a substantive, specific answer.
- Skipping the discovery sub-flow because you've shipped something *adjacent* to this domain before — adjacent fluency is exactly what makes a domain feel well-known when it isn't (see the loyalty-program worked example above).
- Treating a mock-discovery 5/5 as proof of real-world completeness on a domain you already judged niche — it proves internal consistency only.
- Building one large domain document across multiple portals or unrelated-lifecycle entities instead of splitting early, then discovering the shallow-session symptom partway through a long, unfocused session.

## Harborview in practice

Harborview's domain — B2B invoice payment — was judged well-known enough to skip the discovery sub-flow entirely: [`domain.example.md`](../projects/TEMPLATE/domain/domain.example.md) was generated straight from AI training knowledge, no playbook or live session behind it. Run the self-test against it and see why: three consequential rules (line-item requirement, immutable reference numbers, integer-only money handling) are things any experienced delivery PO can state confidently; a wrong assumption in invoicing generally costs a workflow patch, not a schema rebuild; and standard B2B invoicing carries no domain-specific regulator exposure beyond the general audit-trail and retention obligations already covered as universal. All three clear — that's what "skip the sub-flow" is supposed to look like when it's actually justified, not just assumed.

That said, `domain-discovery-state.example.md` *does* model the sub-flow in progress — a deliberately incomplete, 55%-confidence, two-session state file for the same Invoice Payment domain, showing what a real "PO stated:" distillation and a real AI Knowledge Correction (the Overdue vs. Overdue (Escalated) split) look like end to end. Read `domain.example.md` for the target shape of a finished document; read `domain-discovery-state.example.md` for what the process looks like when you do need it, even on a domain that — in Harborview's actual case — turned out not to.
