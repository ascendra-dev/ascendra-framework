What an epic is, and how the two commands split the work

What is an epic? It's the layer between the BRD and stories — a cohesive, independently-shippable capability, typically 4 to 10 stories' worth of work. /gen-stories reads only two things from it: the In Scope bullets and the Notes section. Nothing about how a story gets broken down comes from anywhere else.

Two commands, two different jobs. /gen-epics proposes the breakdown and writes the files. /review-epics is the rigorous per-requirement audit — 13 structural checks, run one epic at a time against the real file, the BRD, and the domain knowledge document, all loaded in full. Knowing which one is supposed to catch which kind of mistake is most of what this chapter teaches — see Don't do /review-epics's job at the planning table below for exactly where that line falls.

Where does an epic's content come from? Entirely from the approved BRD — every REQ, every dependency, every Section 8 note traces back to something already confirmed there. Epics carry no architecture dependency at all. They're written before any technology decision is made, which is exactly why the whole approved BRD can be structured into epics at once, rather than waiting stage by stage.

Decision heuristic

One capability, or two glued together?

Could this feature area ship and be demoed as a complete, independently useful capability on its own — one scenario, one Definition-of-Done walkthrough, no "and separately, at a different time, a different person does X"?The cohesion test — same test as Chapter 3's REQ density, one level up

Four of Harborview's five feature areas map 1:1 to a single epic. Section 5.2 (Invoice Management, eight REQs) is the interesting one — it correctly splits into two epics on the cohesion test: different trigger (create vs. threshold crossed), different acting persona (Finance Manager vs. Director), independently demoable either way.

Don't count REQs and stop there — REQ count is a proxy that catches size outliers, not boundary correctness. A four-REQ epic can still be two capabilities glued together; an eight-REQ epic can still be genuinely one.

Decision heuristic

Don't do /review-epics's job at the planning table

Spend your attention at Step 4 on

Do the groupings feel like real capabilities? Does the dependency order look right at a glance? Is any REQ obviously misfiled?

Don't attempt at Step 4

REQ-by-REQ verbatim checking, In Scope wording precision, Section 8 domain-rule coverage — none of that's possible yet, since no epic file exists to check any of it against.

Decision heuristic

Dependency ordering: the falsifiable test

Epic B depends on Epic A only if a story in B cannot be implemented or meaningfully demoed without a capability A delivers — not merely "A is conceptually foundational."The test

Someone might propose "Dashboard depends on Payer Management — it shows Payer figures." Apply the test: the dashboard's actual figures come from Invoice, Payment, and Overdue state, not a raw Payer record. Payer Management is a real ancestor, but only transitively, through three other epics — not a direct Section 5 dependency. Naming it directly would pass an eyeball check and fail the test.

FW-048 — a stricter constraint than ordinary dependency ordering

Dependency ordering answers "what must exist first." The Walking Skeleton answers "which epic proves the whole system works end to end before anything else gets built to full breadth." A prior real project shipped without one and paid for it: breadth got built across many epics before a single thin path was proven, and nothing downstream could be verified independently as a result.

What to check: every Yes-tagged REQ landed in some epic; that epic is sequenced first in build order, ahead of anything that carries none of the tag; a Walking Skeleton REQ split across two epics is a signal worth pausing on, not automatically wrong.

Mechanism

The story-level interleaving note

Can you name one specific story in the depended-on epic — not the whole epic — that must land before a specific story in the dependent epic can be built or demoed?The trigger test

Harborview never exercises this — every dependency resolves cleanly at the epic level. A hypothetical that does: Staff Invite / Staff Accept-Invite. At the epic level, Accept-Invite depends on all of Staff Invite. The true first blocker is finer — only "generate and email the invite link" has to exist first; revoke, resend, and status-list don't. Scheduling two fully sequential waves would needlessly block Accept-Invite behind capabilities it never touches.

A real, sharp boundary

Section 8 Notes: business rule vs. architecture opinion

✓ Epic-appropriate, full confidence
"Payer data is scoped to the organisation. A Finance Manager from one organisation must not be able to view, edit, or import Payers belonging to another."

A domain/business constraint, true regardless of what architecture turns out to be.

✗ A technical mechanism, stated as settled fact
"...This is enforced by filtering all Payer queries by orgId from the JWT."

A specific implementation mechanism — an auth/tenancy shape decision /gen-architecture hasn't made yet. No check anywhere catches this; hold it loosely as a labeled hint, not fixed fact.

A sanity check, never a commitment

/review-epics's "approximately N stories" has no formula behind it anywhere in the command file. Cross-reference it against the ~4–10-stories-per-epic rule of thumb; if the AI can't give a reason for an outlier when you ask, discount the number to zero. Nothing about sprint capacity should be decided off it.

Tier 3 — what "PO decides" actually asks of you
Reassign a REQ to a different epic only if that requirement's acceptance criteria would naturally be written and tested as part of the OTHER epic's capability — not merely because it feels thematically closer.The test

The four example targeted-PO-questions in /review-epics are explicitly generic and interchangeable — real, but a checklist item wearing a question mark. A sharpened version interrogates a specific state transition the REQ text doesn't address, and couldn't be pasted verbatim into a different epic's review. That non-transferability is the actual test for whether a question is doing its job.

Terminology recap
  • Epic Size / No Gold-PlatingEpic Size flags a REQ-count outlier against siblings. No Gold-Plating flags scope not traced to any REQ or approved domain rule.
  • Story-Level InterleavingA note marking a dependency pair whose true build order is finer than epic-before-epic.
  • Fix tiers (Tier 1–4)/review-epics's own system: Tier 1 applies silently, Tier 2 confirms before applying, Tier 3 is a PO decision with no automatic action, Tier 4 halts the review entirely.
Common mistakes ▸
  • Treating REQ count alone as the sizing test.
  • Doing /review-epics's verification work at the planning table, before material exists to check it against.
  • Listing a dependency because an epic "feels foundational" instead of naming the blocked story.
  • Letting a Walking Skeleton REQ land wherever's convenient without checking sequencing.
  • Treating every dependency as fully sequential when the real blocker is one story.
  • Letting a Section 8 technical hint quietly become a fixed architecture decision in your head.
  • Treating "approximately N stories" as a sprint-planning commitment.
  • Reassigning a REQ for thematic fit instead of acceptance-criteria home.
  • Asking a generic targeted question verbatim instead of sharpening it to this epic specifically.
