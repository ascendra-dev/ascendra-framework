Domain Discovery

This document holds two kinds of facts. Some are true about the domain no matter which client you're working with. Others are true only for this one client. Domain Discovery is where you sort the two apart. Get it wrong, and the mistake follows you through every later phase — the BRD is told to trust this document, not question it. If none of that makes sense yet, keep reading. This chapter starts from zero.

What a domain is, and where this document's knowledge actually comes from

What is a domain? It is one focused area of business capability. It is not a client's whole business. It is often not even the whole system you are building.

Here is an example. Invoice Payment is a domain. Payroll is a different domain — even for the same client, even inside the same system.

Sometimes one system covers two domains at once. A school system might have an admin console and a parent-facing app. Those are two different domains. They need two different domain documents, not one large one. We cover exactly how to tell them apart later, in Splitting a domain.

Why does this need its own stage? Why not just ask the client everything during BRD discovery instead?

Here is the reason. Every project needs a domain knowledge document before BRD discovery can start. The command that builds the BRD playbook, /gen-brd-playbook, will not run without one.

Once the domain document is written, it becomes trusted fact. The BRD is told: do not ask the client about anything already covered in this document. Do not even restate it, unless the client says something that contradicts it.

This is exactly why getting it right here matters so much. A mistake made in this stage does not get caught later. Every later stage is told to stop checking it.

Where do the entities, rules, and validations in this document actually come from? This is worth understanding clearly, because it tells you how much to trust what you're reading.

There are two sources. The command that writes this document has a simple rule for choosing between them: use the PO's own answers where they exist. Where the PO hasn't answered, use Claude's training knowledge instead. If the two ever disagree, the PO's answer always wins.

Here is what that means in practice. Claude has seen an enormous number of real invoicing systems, payroll systems, and HR systems during training. For a well-known domain like these, Claude can sketch a solid starting model from memory — the entities, the usual lifecycle, the common rules — much like an experienced consultant could, without looking anything up. That is recall, not guesswork. For a well-worn domain, recall alone is usually good enough.

But for a niche, regulated, or unfamiliar domain, recall alone is not good enough. That is exactly what the discovery sub-flow is for. Claude says its recalled model out loud, section by section. A real person — you, or a domain expert — confirms it, corrects it, or extends it. Nothing gets written down as settled fact until that happens.

The mechanism, not just the choice

The two paths, concretely

There are two ways to get from an approved brief to a finished domain knowledge document. The difference is not cosmetic. It comes down to which of the two knowledge sources above actually wrote the document.

Path one: skip the sub-flow. /gen-domain-knowledge writes the whole document in one pass, using training knowledge alone. It then shows you the finished result to review, before saving it.

Path two: run the sub-flow. First, /gen-domain-playbook turns the domain into a structured set of questions. Next, /run-domain-discovery actually asks you those questions, live, and records your answers. Only after that does /gen-domain-knowledge write the document — this time using your answers as the main source, and filling in only what you didn't cover from training knowledge.

Both paths end at the same command, and produce the same kind of file. What's different is everything that happens before it.

Decision heuristic

Which path — the central decision

/gen-domain-knowledge will happily write a document from training knowledge alone. For a lot of domains, that's the right call. Running a full discovery session on something Claude already knows well just wastes a session.

But how do you know if your domain is one of those? The command's own warning gives only two rough buckets: "well-known" (invoicing, payroll, school fees, HR) or "niche, regulated, or unfamiliar." Most real domains don't announce which bucket they belong to. "HR" sounds well-known — right up until it turns out to mean HR leave accrual with jurisdiction-specific statutory rules. That's a completely different, much harder domain.

If you answer "no" or "unsure" to even one question below, run the sub-flow. All three need to be a confident "yes" before you skip it.Stricter than it sounds, on purpose
  1. Can you name the three most consequential business rules in this domain from memory, with real confidence — not "I think so"?
  2. If you guess wrong here, would it cost more than a sprint to fix, once the wrong guess has spread into entities, lifecycle states, and BRD requirements?
  3. Is there a regulator, auditor, or legal requirement that would reject wrong output — not just annoy a user, but create real exposure?

This is stricter than it sounds, on purpose. Running an unnecessary discovery session costs one afternoon. Skipping a necessary one costs something much worse: a domain document that looks trustworthy but isn't — and every later phase will trust it anyway.

Worked across the spectrum

Internal expense reimbursement tool — skip the sub-flow

This one should skip the sub-flow. Can you name three rules from memory, with real confidence? Yes: a receipt is required above a threshold, there's a manager-approval chain, and reimbursement is capped at the receipt amount. These are stable, well-known patterns. Would a wrong guess cost more than a sprint to fix? No — this is a small, internal tool. A wrong workflow assumption here is just a config change, not a rebuild. Is a regulator watching? Generally not, for a purely internal, domestic tool. All three answers are clear, so this domain goes straight to /gen-domain-knowledge, using AI knowledge alone.

Retail loyalty & points program — genuinely tricky, walk it through

This one is genuinely tricky. You probably know the general concepts: points build up as customers spend, tiers unlock perks, and points expire eventually. But can you say, with real confidence, exactly when points expire? Is it a rolling window from the last purchase, or a fixed date each year? Does tier status get recalculated every month, or once a year? What happens to points on a refunded order? Most POs find that at least one of these is a guess, not a known fact. A wrong guess here can easily cost more than a sprint — if the expiry rule gets written down as a fixed, universal rule instead of a variation to confirm, fixing it later means changing the points-ledger schema and every calculation built on top of it. Regulator exposure is a real "maybe" too — several places treat unused points as an accounting or unclaimed-property question. So this domain fails at least one of the three questions, often two, even though a PO who has shipped e-commerce before will feel like they already know loyalty programs. That feeling is exactly the trap this test is designed to catch: knowing the general shape of something is not the same as being able to state its actual rule.

Healthcare compliance or cross-border payments — clearly niche, run it

Can you name three rules from memory, confidently? No. Most POs cannot state HIPAA's minimum-necessary-use boundary, or the exact threshold that triggers enhanced checks on a cross-border payment, without looking it up. Would a wrong guess cost more than a sprint? Yes, clearly — these facts get built into entities, lifecycle rules, and audit requirements that spread through the whole architecture. Is a regulator watching? Yes, explicitly. Run the full sub-flow here. And once you've run it, don't stop at 70% confidence — see Confidence score below for why.

What actually happens, if you run it

The sub-flow session, walked through

The sub-flow does not ask open-ended questions with no starting point. Instead, Claude proposes an answer first. You then confirm it, correct it, or add to it. This happens one section at a time. Here is a real example — the actual opening of the Core Entities section, from the playbook generated for Harborview's invoicing domain:

"Based on my knowledge of the Invoice Payment domain, the core entities are: Invoice, Line Item, Payer, and Approval Record. Does this match your understanding? Are there entities I'm missing, or entities on this list that don't apply?"The actual opening prompt, verbatim

The same pattern repeats for every section, in this order: entities, lifecycle states, universal business rules, standard validations, personas, integration points, and the regulatory baseline. Then comes the most important section of all — Known Variations. We explain exactly why it matters most further down this page.

As you finish each section, it gets marked Covered. A running Domain Confidence Score updates after every section too. Your answers are saved as you go, not just at the end.

This session does not have to happen in one sitting. You can stop and come back later. When you resume, it reloads everything you already said and picks up exactly where you left off.

Read past the label

What a good answer looks like, versus a rubber stamp

"Covered," in the discovery state file, only means a section was addressed. It does not mean it was addressed well. A rushed "yes, that all sounds right" from a tired PO gets marked Covered — exactly the same as a detailed, well-reasoned answer. But the two are worth very different amounts. Here they are, side by side.

✗ A rubber stamp
"Yes, that's right."

This technically counts as "Covered." But there's no reasoning here. If a question about it comes up three phases later, there's nothing to check it against.

✓ A real correction — the actual Harborview session
"Overdue and Overdue (Escalated) must be distinct states. Overdue fires automatically when the due date passes with no payment. Overdue (Escalated) fires when the dunning sequence completes with no payment — signalling that automated follow-up is exhausted and Finance Manager manual action is required."

Claude had proposed just one "Overdue" state. The PO corrected it, and explained why: the system needs to tell "still chasing automatically" apart from "needs a human to step in now." That explanation is what makes the correction trustworthy enough to actually build a lifecycle on.

Build this habit: before you trust a section marked "Covered," open it up. Look for a real "PO stated:" paragraph — actual reasoning, in the PO's own words. If all you find is a one-line rubber stamp, treat that section as still open, no matter what the label says.

"Unverified" is a different kind of gap, found in the document's Factual Accuracy Log. It marks a fact — usually something regulatory or time-sensitive — that Claude could not confirm against a real source. Here's the problem: that label does nothing on its own. Nothing stops the document from being used while an Unverified fact sits there. The closing report doesn't even count these for you, the way it counts Open Issues. So you have to check this log yourself, every time. Nothing else will flag it for you.

The highest-stakes call in this phase

Section 5 (Universal Business Rules) vs. Section 10 (Known Variations)

Here is why this one choice matters so much. /gen-brd-playbook builds its entire set of client questions from Section 10 alone. Every Known Variation gets turned into at least one real question. Sections 5 and 6 generate zero questions. That's by design — a Universal Business Rule is, by definition, something you never ask the client about.

Here is what that failure actually looks like. Say you classify "invoices above a threshold need Director approval" as a Universal Business Rule, instead of a Known Variation. This is an easy mistake to make — a lot of B2B invoicing systems do have some approval step.

Now watch what happens next. /gen-brd-playbook never generates a question about approval, because Section 5 never feeds into question generation at all. The real client discovery session finishes without anyone ever asking: "does your organization require approval before sending an invoice?"

If this client doesn't actually need approval, you might never find out — unless they happen to mention it themselves. And if they do want approval, but shaped differently — one approver instead of several, a different threshold — there's no mechanism that ever asks. The BRD gets written against a fact that was never true for this client.

When you are not certain a rule is universal, put it in Section 10. A variation that turns out to be universal costs one redundant question. A Universal Business Rule that turns out to be variable may never surface as a missed requirement at all.The rule

This is not just a hypothetical risk. It nearly happened for real, in the very example this guide uses. Harborview's own domain document once had this Universal Business Rule: "VAT is applied to the invoice subtotal, not to individual line items." But the same document's Section 10 already listed "multiple rates per line item type" as a real option a client could choose. Once a client actually has different VAT rates on different line items, that rule doesn't just become debatable — it gives the wrong number.

A later /judgment-check pass caught this. The rule was rewritten to state only what's truly universal: the total equals the subtotal plus VAT. How VAT itself gets calculated was left to depend on whichever rate model Section 10 confirms.

Nothing had to change downstream this time, because Harborview's own BRD only ever used the simple, single-rate case. But a different client, choosing the per-line-item option, would have hit a rule that was quietly wrong the entire time.

Decision heuristic

The generalization filter

The Domain Scope section asks you to do something genuinely hard: separate what's universal about the domain from what's really just this client's own product decision, hiding inside the brief. Here is the pattern to look for. If a number, a named tier, or a specific policy choice could plausibly be different for another client in the same domain, it's a variation — even when it's written in a sentence that sounds universal.

Brief saysUniversal concept (domain doc)Variation (Known Variations)
"Tiered template options with full customization"Invoice templating capabilityThe tiering/customization model
"Points expire 12 months after last purchase, rolling"Points expiry policy exists12-month rolling, last-purchase-triggered (vs. calendar-year, tier-dependent, none)
"1.5 days/month, capped at 18 days carried forward"Leave accrual mechanism, carry-forward capabilityThe specific rate and cap

Here is the first case, in full. The brief says "tiered template options with full customization." What's universal here is just the general idea of invoice templating — every system in this domain needs some way to format an invoice. The specific tiering model is not a domain fact. It's this client's own product decision, probably tied to their pricing plan. So the filter splits it: "invoice templating capability" goes into Domain Scope. "Tiering and customization model" goes into Known Variations, as something to confirm with this client directly. Neither part gets silently dropped, and neither gets silently treated as universal.

Here is the second case. The brief says points expire 12 months after a customer's last purchase, and the window rolls forward with every new purchase. What's universal is just the idea that points expire at all — nearly every points program has some expiry rule. The specific mechanic — a rolling 12-month window based on last purchase, rather than a fixed date each year, or no expiry — is this client's own design choice. "Points expiry policy" goes into the domain document's scope. "12-month rolling, last-purchase-triggered" goes into Known Variations, next to the other common options.

Here is the third case. The brief says employees earn 1.5 days of leave per month, capped at 18 carried-forward days. What's universal is just "leave accrual" as a concept — every leave-management domain has some accrual mechanism, and one truth that always holds: you can't use leave before you've earned it. The specific rate, the monthly cadence, and the 18-day cap are this client's own policy, not a domain fact. Different clients, in different countries with different legal minimums, will have completely different numbers. "Leave accrual mechanism" and "carry-forward capability" go into scope. The specific rate and cap go into Known Variations.

Decision heuristic

Splitting a domain — when to do it before you start

Split a domain into more than one document when it covers more than one distinct user-facing portal, or more than one independent area of capability. "Admin portal vs. parent portal" is the easy version of this call. Most real cases are harder to see. Is Loyalty & Rewards its own domain, separate from Order Management? Is Dunning its own domain, separate from Invoicing?

Here are two practical tests. Use the first before you start. Use the second to catch it if you missed it.

Before you start — the 2–3 sentence test

Try to describe the domain's scope in 2–3 sentences. Don't join two user roles, or two unrelated entities, with the word "and." If you can't avoid it — "...covers order management and loyalty point redemption..." — that "and" is usually the real seam. Split there.

After you've started — the shallow-session tell

If a session is running long, and the answers feel thin or generic rather than sharp and specific, that's a sign you split too late. A well-scoped domain produces confident, detailed answers, because you're reasoning about one coherent thing. Thin answers over a long session usually mean you're actually asking about two domains at once. Stop. Split it. Run two focused sessions instead.

Scores you can't verify

Nobody publishes the formula behind the Domain Confidence Score. In the worked example, 6 of 10 sections are marked Covered, and the score reads 55%. A simple ratio would give 60%, so it isn't a flat average. Reading the file itself gives a clue: it calls Known Variations "a critical gap," specifically because that section feeds the BRD playbook. The most likely explanation is that this one section is weighted more heavily than the others. But that's a guess on our part, not a documented fact — there's no published formula to check it against.

So treat any score as a rough signal, nothing more. Before you trust the document, read Known Variations and the Regulatory Baseline yourself, in full — no matter what the percentage says.

For a regulated or high-stakes domain, 70% is not good enough — and nothing in the tool will warn you about that. Set your own floor at 90% for these domains. Read the Regulatory Baseline and the Factual Accuracy Log line by line before you treat the document as ready for production.

The blind spot

/run-mock-discovery has one real weakness: the model grading the session is the same model that wrote everything being graded. A perfect 5/5 score proves the domain document and the BRD playbook agree with each other. It does not prove either one is actually complete against the real world. If Claude never thought of a real variation, it won't think to test for it either — same blind spot, checking itself.

Common mistakes ▸
  • Treating "HR" or "invoicing" as automatically well-known without running the three-question test.
  • Classifying an uncertain rule as universal because it "sounds like it's always true."
  • Trusting a confidence score without reading Known Variations yourself — and on regulated domains, the Regulatory Baseline and Factual Accuracy Log too.
  • Marking a discovery-state section "Covered" on a blanket "yes to all" rather than a real, reasoned answer.
  • Skipping the sub-flow because you've shipped something adjacent before. Adjacent fluency is exactly what makes a domain feel known when it isn't.
  • Treating a mock-discovery 5/5 as proof of real-world completeness, rather than just internal consistency.
  • Building one large domain document across multiple portals or unrelated entities, instead of splitting early — then hitting the shallow-session symptom partway through a long, unfocused session.
