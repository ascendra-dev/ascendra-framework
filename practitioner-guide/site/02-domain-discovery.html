Domain Discovery

The layer that separates "true about this domain in every implementation" from "true about this specific client." Get the separation wrong and a bad assumption compounds through every phase after it — the BRD doesn't re-ask what domain knowledge already claims is settled.

One correction to SDLC.md's framing, worth flagging up front: the AI-knowledge-only path is not "skip domain knowledge" — /gen-domain-knowledge itself is never optional, since /gen-brd-playbook requires its output to exist. What's optional is only the discovery sub-flow that feeds it.

Decision heuristic

The central decision: do you need the sub-flow?

Any single "no" or "unsure" answer means run the sub-flow. All three need to be a confident yes to skip straight to AI-knowledge-only.Stricter than it sounds, on purpose
  1. Can you name the three most consequential business rules in this domain from memory, with real confidence?
  2. Would a wrong assumption cost more than a sprint to unwind once propagated downstream?
  3. Is there a regulator, auditor, or legal requirement that would reject wrong output?

Worked across the spectrum

Expense reimbursement — skip

Stable, well-worn rules named confidently. Small blast radius. No regulator exposure. All three clear.

Retail loyalty points — walk it through

You can name the concepts but not state expiry model or refund handling with confidence. Partial fluency reads as confidence until you're asked to state the actual rule — that's the trap.

Healthcare / cross-border payments — run it

Most POs can't state the actual regulatory thresholds. Embeds into entities and audit requirements. Regulator exposure by design.

The highest-stakes call in this phase

Section 5 (Universal Business Rules) vs. Section 10 (Known Variations)

/gen-brd-playbook generates its entire discovery-question set only from Section 10. Sections 5 and 6 generate zero questions — by design, since a Universal Business Rule is defined as something you don't ask the client about. Misclassify a real variation as universal, and the client discovery session runs to completion without anyone ever asking about it.

When you are not certain a rule is universal, put it in Section 10. A variation that turns out to be universal costs one redundant question. A Universal Business Rule that turns out to be variable costs a missed requirement that will not reliably surface on its own.The rule
Decision heuristic

The generalization filter

Separate genuinely universal domain concepts from this-client's-specific product decisions sitting in the brief. The pattern across every worked example: if a number, a named tier, or a specific policy choice could plausibly be different for another client in the same domain, it's a variation wearing a universal-sounding sentence.

Decision heuristic

Sub-domain decomposition — when to split before you start

Before you start

Try describing the domain's scope in 2–3 sentences without joining two user roles or two lifecycle-independent entities with "and." If the "and" is doing real work, that's the seam — split there.

After you've started

If a long session's answers feel thin or generic rather than sharp, that's the domain telling you it's actually two domains being asked about at once. Stop, split, run two focused sessions.

Read past the label

"Covered" and "Unverified" aren't what they sound like

"Covered" means a section was addressed — not addressed well. A rushed "yes, that all sounds right" gets marked Covered exactly like a detailed answer. Check whether there's a substantive "PO stated:" paragraph behind it, or just a one-line rubber stamp.

"Unverified" (Section 12.3) marks a fact the AI couldn't confirm against an authoritative source — but nothing blocks the document from being used downstream while it sits there, and the closing report doesn't surface an "Unverified: N" count. You have to personally scan Section 12.3 every time.

Scores you can't verify

The Domain Confidence Score's formula is not published. The worked example shows 55% after 6 of 10 sections — a naive ratio would say 60%, so it isn't a flat average. Treat any score as a rough directional signal: read Section 12 (Known Variations) and Section 9 (Regulatory Baseline) yourself, regardless of the percentage.

70% is not sufficient for a regulated or high-stakes domain, and nothing raises it for you automatically. Apply a personal floor of 90% for those domains, and re-read Section 9 and 12.3 line by line before treating the document as production-ready.

The blind spot

/run-mock-discovery's grading model is the same model that wrote the material being graded. A 5/5 pass proves the domain document and BRD playbook are internally consistent with each other — it is not evidence they're complete against the real world. A gap in Section 10 the AI simply didn't think to include won't get probed for either, for the same underlying reason it wasn't written in the first place.

Common mistakes ▸
  • Treating "HR" or "invoicing" as automatically well-known without running the three-question test.
  • Classifying an uncertain rule as universal because it "sounds like it's always true."
  • Trusting a confidence score without personally reading Section 12, and on regulated domains, Section 9 and 12.3.
  • Marking a discovery-state section "Covered" on a blanket "yes to all."
  • Skipping the sub-flow because you've shipped something adjacent before — adjacent fluency is exactly what makes a domain feel known when it isn't.
  • Treating a mock-discovery 5/5 as proof of real-world completeness rather than internal consistency.
